U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) was created in March 2003 after the 9/11 attacks as part of the Department of Homeland Security. Its Enforcement and Removal Operations (ERO) unit carries out arrests, detention and deportations inside the United States — not at the border.

In the second Trump administration (from January 2025), ICE operations expanded rapidly. The 2025 "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" provided $170 billion in new enforcement funding. Detention capacity rose from roughly 40,000 to over 70,000 people — the highest in ICE history. Operations now target not only recent arrivals but also long-term residents with no criminal record.

The 287(g) programme trains and authorises local police officers to carry out immigration enforcement tasks — for example, checking a person's immigration status during a routine traffic stop. It now covers counties that are home to 32% of the U.S. population.

Official figures report more than 675,000 formal deportations in the first year, plus approximately 2.2 million self-deportations (people who left voluntarily due to increased enforcement pressure). Independent sources (TRAC, MPI, Reuters) estimate 540,000–622,000 total removals, with interior — non-border — arrests rising sharply.

Roughly one-third of new detainees have no criminal conviction; they are held for civil immigration violations such as overstaying a visa or entering without inspection. The remaining two-thirds have criminal records — common examples include DUI, drug possession, theft, or assault.

02

The Town-Hall Debate

Preparation · Opening statements · Discussion · Creative solutions · Exit — 45 minutes

Tonight's Motion

"Our town has been told an ICE raid is coming next month. Should local police fully cooperate, cooperate only for serious criminals, refuse entirely, or propose a creative alternative?"

Full timeline · 45 min
0 – 10 min · Group Work

Role Preparation in Groups

  • Re-read your role card — highlight the two most powerful arguments.
  • Write a key-word outline for your 60-second opening statement.
  • Choose at least 4 vocabulary terms you will use in the debate.
  • Plan one pointed question to challenge another role's position.
10 – 20 min · Plenary

60-Second Opening Statements

  • Each role delivers a 60-second uninterrupted statement.
  • Listeners take brief notes: What is their main claim? Which fact do they use?
  • Order: Officer → Worker → Farmer → Activist → Sheriff → Lawyer → Economist
20 – 37 min · Town-Hall Discussion

Open Floor — The Town-Hall

  • Chair (your teacher) moderates — anyone may speak, but must use a phrase card formula.
  • If another role challenges you directly, you must respond.
  • You must use at least 4 key vocabulary words with correct context.
  • At approx. min 28: Chair announces — "10 minutes left. Move toward a decision."
37 – 43 min · Solutions Round

Creative Solutions — The Compromise Roundtable

  • Each group proposes one concrete compromise using the frame: "We propose… because… The benefit for all sides is…"
  • The room votes (show of hands) on the most realistic proposal.
43 – 45 min · Individual

Exit Ticket

Three reflections — see below. Honest answers only.

Useful Debate Phrases

Exit Round — Oral Reflection

Take 2 minutes to think quietly, then share one of these with the group. There are no wrong answers — honest reflection matters more than a perfect sentence.

  • 1
    Name one fact from today that genuinely surprised you. Think: What did you not know before? Why does it matter?
  • 2
    Which argument from a different role did you find most convincing — and why? Try to name the role and the specific reason it was persuasive.
  • 3
    Has your own position changed after the debate? Say what you think now — and what changed your mind. It is perfectly fine to say your view stayed the same — but explain why.

Follow-Up Writing Task — Blog Entry

Choose one prompt below and write a blog entry (approx. 200–250 words). Remember: catchy title, personal tone, address your readers directly, and end with a call to action.

Prompt 1
"I Used to Think…"

Write about how the debate changed — or confirmed — your view on ICE enforcement. Start with what you believed before and explain what shifted, and why.

Prompt 2
"A Day in [Your Role]'s Shoes"

Write as your debate character. Describe one day in their life during the weeks before the ICE raid. Make the reader feel the tension and uncertainty.

Prompt 3
"The Argument That Stopped Me"

Blog about the single most convincing argument you heard from another role during the debate. Why did it hit harder than you expected?

Prompt 4
"What Would I Actually Do?"

If you were the sheriff of this town, what would you decide — and how would you explain it to your community? Write the blog post you'd publish the day after the town-hall.

Prompt 5
"Numbers Don't Lie — Or Do They?"

Pick two statistics from the reading (e.g. 70,000 detainees; 1 in 3 with no criminal record). Write a blog post that unpacks what those numbers really mean for real people.

Structure reminder: Catchy title  ·  Greeting & hook  ·  Body paragraphs  ·  Take-home message  ·  Call to action ("Leave a comment!", "Share your story!")
